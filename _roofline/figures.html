Which decoder does the roofline model make faster?

Six settings of model, accelerator, batch and context. For each, decide whether block diffusion (TD) or autoregressive decoding (AR) has the higher throughput under the roofline model. Each answer comes with the roofline that decides it, and the rest of the page explains why.

How the numbers are counted

Every gain on this page is a ratio of roofline bounds, counted in one of three ways.

A roofline time is a lower bound on the time of a pass, so each throughput is an upper bound, and \(\Gamma\) is the ratio of two such bounds, not a measurement. In vLLM, TD reaches 84% of its bound at batch 1 and 34% at batch 64 (appendix). The three accountings below differ in what they include, and each number on the page says which one it uses. In the interactive figures and the cards, the intensities \(I_{AR}\) and \(I_{TD}\) are always those of Definition 3, without the vocabulary; next to an exact gain, the regime of a pass is given instead as the share of the peak compute that it uses. The cards quote the closed-form values of the propositions, at zero context where §3 does, unless a readout says exact. A fourth variant, the kernel-by-kernel timing rule of §5, can be switched on in Figure 3 and in the calculator.

Figure 1 · interactive

TD does more work per token, but reads each byte for more tokens.

Each slot is one forward pass, and every pass reads the weights once. Press play, change the canvas length \(b\) and the number of denoising passes \(S\), then switch the model from dense to MoE.

One sequence, batch 1, short context, and both decoders assumed memory-bound, so that a pass takes the time to read its weights; this fails for a dense model once the canvas is longer than about \(I^\star\) positions, where Corollary 1 caps the gain. A canvas costs \(\omega = S + 1\) passes (here \(r = 1\)) and commits \(b\) tokens; in the same \(\omega\) passes AR commits \(\omega\) tokens. For a dense model both passes read the same weights, and per committed token TD reads \(\omega/b = 1/\mathrm{TPF}\) times the bytes of AR: \(\Gamma = \mathrm{TPF}\). For an MoE model a canvas touches more experts than one token does, a TD pass reads \(\Pi_{\mathrm{lin}}\) times the weights of an AR step (the two bars, Proposition 3), and \(\Gamma = \mathrm{TPF}/\Pi_{\mathrm{lin}}\).

Figure 3 · interactive

The best batch is the one at which a TD pass reaches the ridge.

The curve is hidden. Drag the marker to the batch at which you expect TD's lead over AR to be largest, then reveal it.

Exact roofline gain \(\Gamma(B)\) at a fixed context of 1,024: DiffusionGemma, BF16, \(b = 256\), \(S = 14\), vocabulary included. Under (A1), left of \(B^\star_{TD}\) both decoders are memory-bound and \(\Gamma \approx \mathrm{TPF}/\Pi_{\mathrm{lin}}(B)\) (Proposition 3, without the vocabulary) rises as the batch dilutes the MoE penalty. Right of it a TD pass is compute-bound and \(\Gamma \approx I^\star/(\omega I_{AR})\) falls, roughly like \(1/B\) while the weights dominate AR's bytes, down to parity at \(B_\times\) where there is one. Over integer batches the maximum is the last memory-bound batch or the first compute-bound one. With the kernels of a pass timed one after another instead (the remark of §5), the dense layers of a TD pass are compute-bound before its experts are, and the peak no longer sits at a single transition: it is lower on every accelerator here, and on most of them it comes later (batch 9 instead of 5 on an H200). Dashed: the model, the batch's KV cache and one canvas of logits do not fit in the accelerator's memory.

Figure 4 · interactive

As the context grows, both intensities forget the batch.

Each curve is the intensity of a pass at one batch size: blue for an AR step, orange for a TD pass. Drag along the context axis, and change the accelerator to move the ridge.

Intensities of Definition 3 (no vocabulary): DiffusionGemma, BF16, \(b = 256\). The weights are shared by the batch and the KV cache is not, so as the cache takes over the bytes, each family of curves merges: \(I_{AR}\) tends to \(I_{\mathrm{attn}} = 8\), below every ridge point of this note, and \(I_{TD}\) to \(b\,I_{\mathrm{attn}} = 2{,}048\), above every one. Under Proposition 1 the gain then tends to \(I^\star/(\omega I_{\mathrm{attn}})\) whatever the batch, 1.72 on an H200. At 1M context the AR curves are within a factor 1.3 of each other. The TD curves are still a factor 3 apart, which no longer changes the gain once they are all above the ridge. Dashed: the model, the batch's KV cache and one canvas of logits do not fit in the accelerator's memory.

Figure 6 · interactive

The gain depends on the accelerator only through its ridge point, and a higher ridge never lowers it.

The curve is the gain for a chip of ridge point \(I^\star = \pi/\beta\); each dot is an accelerator. Drag the marker along the axis, select a dot, or switch to long context.

Exact roofline gains, DiffusionGemma, BF16, \(b = 256\), \(S = 14\). Multiplying the peak \(\pi\) and the bandwidth \(\beta\) by the same factor multiplies the time of every pass alike, so \(\Gamma\) depends on the accelerator only through \(I^\star\), and the accelerators lie on the curve. Short context: \(C = 1{,}024\), at the batch that maximises \(\Gamma\); on the flat stretch near \(I^\star = 60\), batch 1 is best and both decoders are memory-bound, so the gain is \(\mathrm{TPF}/\Pi\) whatever the ridge. Long context: \(C = 2^{20}\) at batch 1; the dashed line is the limit \(I^\star/(\omega I_{\mathrm{attn}})\) as \(C \to \infty\), linear in \(I^\star\). Over this range the curve lies above the line, because at batch 1 the weights are still about a quarter of an AR step's bytes; above \(I^\star \approx 620\) a TD pass at batch 1 is itself memory-bound at this context, and the curve is flat. The curve assumes unlimited memory; a hollow dot marks an accelerator in which the model and its KV cache do not fit.
