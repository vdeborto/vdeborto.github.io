Our main throughput result

We start by giving our main result, which is a compact formula for the throughput gain of TD over AR. Of course, this formula is *idealized*, as it holds under some critical assumptions, but we will see that it can faithfully act as a guiding north star while navigating TD and AR inference. You can click on any formula to see a definition of the symbols it uses. In this chapter, we first lay down the setup and discuss the concept of arithmetic intensities, see [Pope et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.05102) for a more thorough introduction to the topic. Next, we derive the throughput gains of TD versus AR and discuss a few consequences of our main result.

The setup

What do we need to track?

In order for us to state our main result (Proposition 1), we need to define the key quantities which govern the inference throughput of AR and TD. These quantities determine whether moving bytes (weights and KV cache) or performing actual work (matrix multiplies) is the limiting factor, i.e., whether the model is memory-bound or compute-bound. In order to do so, we need to compute the FLOPs of a forward pass of an AR model and those of a TD model. Similarly, we need to compute how many bytes are moved during a forward pass of the AR and TD models. By doing so, we will be able to establish the arithmetic intensities of both models, i.e., to answer the question: "how many FLOPs are used for each byte that is moved by the model?". In the previous section, we have given the setup to obtain a closed simplified formula for the throughput gains of TD over AR in Proposition 1. Then we have turned to two different regimes which arise at different context length for both AR and TD. In this final section, before diving into the practice of AR and TD inference engines, we provide here a calculator to illustrate the throughput gains of TD over AR. In particular, our calculator accounts not only for model choices but also for hardware specifications.

Why do we even care?

Text Diffusion (TD) is one of the popular alternatives to traditional Autoregressive (AR) decoding when it comes to generative AI. The field mostly develops around the promise that one can design better algorithms more suited to the hardware at hand, making heavy use of parallel compute. It is fair to say that diffusion Language Models (dLMs), the models that TD decodes, are still mostly in their infancy and that many research questions are still open. We broadly classify them into two categories: * **Capabilities:** Can dLMs deliver intelligent models on par with (or better than) their AR counterparts? Improving the capabilities of native dLMs is an active field of research. It is still unclear which diffusion model approach and representation will yield the best results: continuous/discrete modeling, vocabulary embedding or one-hot token representation, etc. * **Serving speed:** How much faster can a dLM be compared with AR? This discussion is the topic of this blogpost. We will see that it requires a good understanding of rooflines, hardware specifications but also kernel engineering and serving software. Therefore, in the rest of this blogpost, *we do not concern ourselves with discussion on the quality of dLMs*. Our only goal is to identify in which scenarios, both in theory and in practice, dLMs can provide substantial serving gains over AR.

Who is this blogpost for?

The aim of this blogpost is entirely **pedagogical**. I do not claim any significant theoretical or practical novelty. The blogpost is intended for researchers and engineers who are curious about the differences between TD and AR when it comes to serving. I am not an expert on these topics myself, and I hope that this blogpost serves as a gentle introduction for the curious reader.
