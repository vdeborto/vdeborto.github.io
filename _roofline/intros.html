Comparing two decoders on a roofline requires, for each, the FLOPs of a forward pass and the bytes it reads from memory. This chapter defines both for an AR step and for a TD pass over a canvas of \(b\) tokens. From them come the two quantities used in every later chapter: the arithmetic intensity of a pass, which decides whether it is compute- or memory-bound, and the penalty, the ratio of the bytes per sequence that a TD pass reads to those an AR step reads, above 1 because its \(b\) positions touch more experts and, in sparse layers, more cached tokens. With the costs of Part 1, the roofline model bounds the time of each pass, and hence the throughput of each decoder. Proposition 1 gives their ratio: the FLOP rates the two decoders attain, divided by the \(\omega = Sr + 1\) passes that a canvas costs. Corollary 1 reads off which decoder wins, and Proposition 2 splits each intensity into a linear part and an attention part, which Parts 3 and 4 treat separately. At short context a pass reads mostly weights, which the batch shares, so the batch \(B\) sets both intensities. This chapter computes the linear penalty \(\Pi_{\mathrm{lin}}\), the ratio of the weight bytes a canvas reads to those an AR step reads (7.9 for DiffusionGemma at batch 1, falling to 1 as the batch grows), states up to which context the linear layers dominate, and derives the gain in that regime. In particular, the best batch is the one at which a TD pass just becomes compute-bound. At long context a pass reads mostly the KV cache, and every sequence brings its own, so a larger batch no longer raises the intensity of either decoder. A canvas still does: its \(b\) queries share one read of the cache. This chapter gives the costs of dense, sliding-window and sparse attention layers, shows that the selection penalty of a sparse layer is the expert penalty \(\Pi_E\) with the canvas in the role of the batch, and derives the gain when attention dominates. Why do we even care?

Text Diffusion (TD) is one of the popular alternatives to traditional Autoregressive (AR) decoding when it comes to generative AI. The field mostly develops around the promise that one can design better algorithms more suited to the hardware at hand, making heavy use of parallel compute. It is fair to say that diffusion Language Models (dLMs) are still mostly in their infancy and that many research questions are still open. We broadly classify them in two categories: * **Capabilities:** Can dLMs deliver intelligent models on par with (or better than) their AR counterparts? Improving the capabilities of native dLMs is an active field of research. It is still unclear which diffusion model approach and representation will yield the best results: continuous/discrete modeling, vocabulary embedding or one-hot token representation, etc. * **Serving speed:** How much faster can a dLM be compared with AR? This discussion is the topic of this blogpost. We will see that it requires a good understanding of rooflines, hardware specifications but also kernel engineering and serving software. Therefore, in the rest of this blogpost, *we do not concern ourselves with discussion on the quality of dLMs*. Our only goal is to identify in which scenarios, both in theory and in practice, dLMs can provide substantial serving gains over AR.
