Our main throughput result

We start by giving our main result, which is a compact formula for the throughput gain of TD over AR. Of course, this formula is *idealized*, as it holds under some critical assumptions, but we will see that it can faithfully act as a guiding north star while navigating TD and AR inference. What do we need to track?

In order for us to state our main result (Proposition 1), we need to define the key quantities which govern the inference throughput of AR and TD. These quantities determine whether moving bytes (weights and KV cache) or performing actual work (matrix multiplies) is the limiting factor, i.e., whether the model is memory-bound or compute-bound. In order to do so, we need to compute the FLOPs of a forward pass of an AR model and those of a TD model. Similarly, we need to compute how many bytes are moved during a forward pass of the AR and TD models. By doing so, we will be able to establish the arithmetic intensities of both models, i.e., "how many FLOPs are used for each byte that is moved by the model?". Given the costs of Part 1, the roofline model bounds the time of each pass, and hence the throughput of each decoder. In this chapter, Proposition 1 gives the ratio of the two throughputs: the ratio of the FLOP rates that the two decoders attain, divided by the \(\omega = Sr + 1\) passes that a canvas costs. We then read off which decoder wins in Corollary 1. Finally, Proposition 2 splits each intensity into a linear part and an attention part, which Parts 3 and 4 treat separately. At short context, a pass reads mostly weights. The batch shares these weights, hence the batch \(B\) sets both intensities. In this chapter, we compute the linear penalty \(\Pi_{\mathrm{lin}}\), i.e., the ratio of the weight bytes a canvas reads to those an AR step reads. For DiffusionGemma, it is 7.9 at batch 1 and falls to 1 as the batch grows. We then state up to which context the linear layers dominate, and derive the gain in that regime. In particular, the best batch is the one at which a TD pass just becomes compute-bound. At long context, a pass reads mostly the KV cache. Every sequence brings its own cache, hence a larger batch no longer raises the intensity of either decoder. On the other hand, a canvas still does: its \(b\) queries share one read of the cache. In this chapter, we give the costs of dense, sliding-window and sparse attention layers. We show that the selection penalty of a sparse layer is the expert penalty \(\Pi_E\) at batch 1, with the cached tokens in the role of the experts. Finally, we derive the gain when attention dominates. Parts 3 and 4 treat the two extremes: a pass that reads only weights, and one that reads only KV cache. In this chapter, we cover every context in between. First, we state when TD wins (Proposition 7) and the batch it needs to saturate compute (Proposition 8). Second, we sort the cases into eight regimes. Finally, we compare accelerators and models, and show the exact gain over batch and context. The propositions give closed forms, but only under simplifying assumptions. Why do we even care?

Text Diffusion (TD) is one of the popular alternatives to traditional Autoregressive (AR) decoding when it comes to generative AI. The field mostly develops around the promise that one can design better algorithms more suited to the hardware at hand, making heavy use of parallel compute. It is fair to say that diffusion Language Models (dLMs) are still mostly in their infancy and that many research questions are still open. We broadly classify them in two categories: * **Capabilities:** Can dLMs deliver intelligent models on par with (or better than) their AR counterparts? Improving the capabilities of native dLMs is an active field of research. It is still unclear which diffusion model approach and representation will yield the best results: continuous/discrete modeling, vocabulary embedding or one-hot token representation, etc. * **Serving speed:** How much faster can a dLM be compared with AR? This discussion is the topic of this blogpost. We will see that it requires a good understanding of rooflines, hardware specifications but also kernel engineering and serving software. Therefore, in the rest of this blogpost, *we do not concern ourselves with discussion on the quality of dLMs*. Our only goal is to identify in which scenarios, both in theory and in practice, dLMs can provide substantial serving gains over AR.
