Our main throughput result

We start by giving our main result which is a compact formula for the throughput gain of TD over AR. Of course, this formula is *idealized* as it works under some critical assumptions but we will see that it can faithfully acts as a guiding North start while navigating TD and AR inference. You can click on any formula to see a definition of the symbols it uses. To compare two decoders on a roofline, we need, for each of them, the FLOPs of a forward pass and the bytes it reads from memory. In this chapter, we define both for an AR step and for a TD pass over a canvas of \(b\) tokens. From them come the two quantities used in every later chapter. First, the arithmetic intensity of a pass decides whether it is compute- or memory-bound. Second, the penalty is the ratio of the bytes per sequence that a TD pass reads to those an AR step reads. It is above 1 because the \(b\) positions of a TD pass touch more experts and, in sparse layers, more cached tokens. Given the costs of Part 1, the roofline model bounds the time of each pass, and hence the throughput of each decoder. In this chapter, Proposition 1 gives the ratio of the two throughputs: the ratio of the FLOP rates that the two decoders attain, divided by the \(\omega = Sr + 1\) passes that a canvas costs. We then read off which decoder wins in Corollary 1. Finally, Proposition 2 splits each intensity into a linear part and an attention part, which Parts 3 and 4 treat separately. At short context, a pass reads mostly weights. The batch shares these weights, hence the batch \(B\) sets both intensities. In this chapter, we compute the linear penalty \(\Pi_{\mathrm{lin}}\), i.e., the ratio of the weight bytes a canvas reads to those an AR step reads. For DiffusionGemma, it is 7.9 at batch 1 and falls to 1 as the batch grows. We then state up to which context the linear layers dominate, and derive the gain in that regime. In particular, the best batch is the one at which a TD pass just becomes compute-bound. At long context, a pass reads mostly the KV cache. Every sequence brings its own cache, hence a larger batch no longer raises the intensity of either decoder. On the other hand, a canvas still does: its \(b\) queries share one read of the cache. In this chapter, we give the costs of dense, sliding-window and sparse attention layers. We show that the selection penalty of a sparse layer is the expert penalty \(\Pi_E\), with the canvas in the role of the batch. Finally, we derive the gain when attention dominates. Parts 3 and 4 treat the two extremes: a pass that reads only weights, and one that reads only KV cache. In this chapter, we cover every context in between. First, we state when TD wins (Proposition 7) and the batch it needs to saturate compute (Proposition 8). Second, we sort the cases into eight regimes. Finally, we compare accelerators and models, and show the exact gain over batch and context. The propositions give closed forms, but only under simplifying assumptions. The calculator below drops these assumptions: it evaluates every pass of a generation with the exact accounting of the appendix, for any accelerator, model, attention layout and decoding setup. For the chosen setting, it reports both throughputs, the AR batch that matches TD, the memory each decoder needs, and the maps of Part 5. In addition, the page address records the setting, so it can be shared. Every number in Parts 1–7 is a roofline bound computed from a cost model, not a measurement; the measurements come in Parts 8–11. In this appendix, we describe the exact accounting behind the maps, the headline numbers and the calculator. We also point to the measurements that check it, and we list the sources of the model shapes and accelerator specifications.

Why do we even care?

Text Diffusion (TD) is one of the popular alternatives to traditional Autoregressive (AR) decoding when it comes to generative AI. The field mostly develops around the promise that one can design better algorithms more suited to the hardware at hand, making heavy use of parallel compute. It is fair to say that diffusion Language Models (dLMs) are still mostly in their infancy and that many research questions are still open. We broadly classify them in two categories: * **Capabilities:** Can dLMs deliver intelligent models on par with (or better than) their AR counterparts? Improving the capabilities of native dLMs is an active field of research. It is still unclear which diffusion model approach and representation will yield the best results: continuous/discrete modeling, vocabulary embedding or one-hot token representation, etc. * **Serving speed:** How much faster can a dLM be compared with AR? This discussion is the topic of this blogpost. We will see that it requires a good understanding of rooflines, hardware specifications but also kernel engineering and serving software. Therefore, in the rest of this blogpost, *we do not concern ourselves with discussion on the quality of dLMs*. Our only goal is to identify in which scenarios, both in theory and in practice, dLMs can provide substantial serving gains over AR.
