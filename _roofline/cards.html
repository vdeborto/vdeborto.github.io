\[ \begin{gathered} \omega = S\,r + 1,\\[4pt] \Gamma = \frac{1}{\omega}\cdot\frac{\min(I_{TD},\,I^\star)}{\min(I_{AR},\,I^\star)}. \end{gathered} \]

The canvas cost \(\omega\) corresponds to \(S\) denoising passes, each one costing \(r\) forward passes (\(r \lt 1\) for a smaller denoiser), and one commit pass that writes the canvas to the KV cache. The intensity of a pass does not depend on \(S\), so under Proposition 1 the gain is proportional to \(1/\omega\).

\[ \begin{gathered} \mathrm{TPF} = \frac{b}{\omega},\qquad I_{TD} = \frac{b}{\Pi}\,I_{AR},\\[4pt] B^\star_{TD} \approx \frac{I^\star s_wW_{TD}}{2b\,N_{\mathrm{act}}}. \end{gathered} \]

While a TD pass is memory-bound, i.e., \(I_{TD} \leq I^\star\), we have that \(\Gamma = \mathrm{TPF}/\Pi\) and therefore the gain grows linearly with \(b\). Once it is compute-bound, \(\Gamma = I^\star/(\omega I_{AR})\) no longer depends on \(b\). Additionally, a longer canvas reaches that regime at a smaller batch[^1].

[^1]: One trade-off that we deliberately leave out of the analysis is that the longer the canvas, the harder it is for TD to reach the quality of AR.
\[ \begin{gathered} \Pi_{\mathrm{lin}}(B) = \frac{W_{TD}(B)}{W_{AR}(B)},\\[4pt] W_{AR}(B) = W_{\mathrm{ne}} + L\,U_{E,k}(B)\,W^e,\\[2pt] W_{TD}(B) = W_{\mathrm{ne}} + L\,U_{E,k}(Bb)\,W^e,\\[4pt] U_{E,k}(N) = E\Bigl[1 - \bigl(1 - \tfrac{k}{E}\bigr)^{N}\Bigr]. \end{gathered} \]

\(U_{E,k}(N)\) is the expected number of distinct experts that \(N\) positions touch in one layer. A TD step routes \(Bb\) positions, and an AR step routes \(B\) positions and touches about \(kB\) experts. The dense part \(W_{\mathrm{ne}}\) is read by both and dilutes the penalty incurred by TD. Dense models have \(\Pi_{\mathrm{lin}} = 1\). The penalty \(\Pi_{\mathrm{lin}}\) divides the gain when both TD and AR are memory-bound. Hence the effect is mostly felt at small batch sizes.

\[ \begin{gathered} \Pi_{\mathrm{attn}}(C) - 1 = \frac{\nu_S\,\bigl[\,U_{C,m}(b) - m\,\bigr]}{Q^{AR}_{\mathrm{attn}}(C)},\\[4pt] U_{C,m}(b) = C\Bigl[1 - \bigl(1 - \tfrac{m}{C}\bigr)^{b}\Bigr] \quad (C \ge m). \end{gathered} \]

This is the counterpart of the linear penalty on the attention side, when the attention is sparse. Each query selects its own \(m\) cached tokens. A TD step reads \(U_{C,m}(b)\) tokens (up to \(\min(bm, C)\)), while an AR step reads only \(m\) (since there is only one query). The penalty is 1 while \(C \le m\), largest for \(m \lt C \lesssim bm\), and returns to 1 as \(C \to \infty\), since the union saturates at \(bm\) while the AR attention bytes grow linearly in \(C\) because of the indexer scan.

\[ Q^{X}(B, C) = \underbrace{\frac{s_wW_X(B)}{B}}_{\text{weights}} + \underbrace{Q^{X}_{\mathrm{attn}}(C)}_{\text{cache}}. \]

For \(X \in \{AR, TD\}\), the weights are shared by the batch and each sequence has its own cache. A larger batch divides the weight bytes per sequence and leaves the cache bytes unchanged. So raising the batch size raises the intensity of both AR and TD. At first it helps TD more, due to the linear MoE penalty. But once TD is compute-bound, it only raises \(I_{AR}\), and \(\Gamma = I^\star/(\omega I_{AR})\) falls.

\[ \begin{gathered} I_X = \theta^X_{\mathrm{lin}}\,I^X_{\mathrm{lin}} + \theta^X_{\mathrm{attn}}\,I^X_{\mathrm{attn}},\\[4pt] \theta^X_{\mathrm{attn}} = \frac{Q^X_{\mathrm{attn}}}{Q^X}. \end{gathered} \]

Each intensity (TD or AR) is the weighted average of its two parts, linear and attention (Proposition 2). As \(C\) grows, the attention share \(\theta^X_{\mathrm{attn}}\) goes to 1 and the intensity of the model is roughly the intensity of the attention. Without sparse layers, the arithmetic intensity of the attention of TD is \(b\) times that of AR, and therefore long contexts favour TD.

Both passes run at the peak, so time is FLOPs, and a canvas spends \(\omega\) times AR's FLOPs per token: \(\Gamma \le 1/\omega\), equal to it under Proposition 1. We claim that this setting is unrealistic, but just how unrealistic is it? AR becomes compute-bound at

\[ \begin{gathered} B^\star_{AR} = \frac{I^\star s_wW_{\mathrm{tot}}}{2N_{\mathrm{act}}}\\[4pt] = \frac{206 \times 2 \times 24.5\text{B}}{2 \times 3.08\text{B}} \approx 1{,}640. \end{gathered} \]

This is with zero context \(C\)! The batch that AR needs grows with the context, and there is usually even a point beyond which no batch size renders AR compute-bound. For instance, by Proposition 8, beyond about 135 tokens of context no batch makes an AR step compute-bound.

Per sequence, an AR step reads \(Q^{AR}\) bytes for one token; a canvas reads \(\omega\) times \(Q^{TD} = \Pi\,Q^{AR}\) for \(b\) tokens. Both passes are memory-bound, so the gain is the ratio of these bytes per token:

\[ \Gamma = \frac{Q^{AR}}{\omega\,\Pi\,Q^{AR}/b} = \frac{\mathrm{TPF}}{\Pi}. \]

TD runs at the peak FLOP rate \(\pi\) and AR at \(I_{AR}\,\beta\), so TD computes \(I^\star/I_{AR}\) times faster than AR but does \(\omega\) times as many FLOPs per token; the ratio of the two is the formula above. At short context, \(I_{AR}\) grows with the batch. At long context, \(I_{AR} \to I_{\mathrm{attn}}\), and at any batch we have

\[ \Gamma \to \frac{I^\star}{\omega\,I_{\mathrm{attn}}} = \frac{206}{15 \times 8} = 1.72. \]
The above suggests that at long context, TD is *(almost) always* better than AR when it comes to throughput.
