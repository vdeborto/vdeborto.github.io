Block Diffusion Rooflines

when block text diffusion beats autoregressive decoding · a short book on its inference cost

Part 0 · IntroductionNext: Summary

This book asks when block text diffusion produces more tokens per second than autoregressive decoding, for the same model on the same accelerator. Parts 1–7 answer with roofline bounds; Parts 8–11 measure the answer for DiffusionGemma on one H200 and account for the difference. This chapter states the question, shows how the parts fit together, and fixes the setting of the measurements.

The question

An autoregressive decoder (AR) runs the model once per generated token. A block text diffusion decoder (TD) writes a canvas of \(b\) positions at once: it refines all of them over \(S\) denoising passes and then commits them in one more pass. Both decoders run the same kind of forward pass over the same weights, so the question is which of them turns a second of accelerator time into more tokens.

Figure 1

In the same number of passes, AR commits one token per pass and TD a whole canvas.

One sequence, DiffusionGemma's setting: a canvas of \(b = 256\) positions, \(S = 14\) denoising passes and one commit pass, so \(\omega = 15\) passes per canvas and \(\mathrm{TPF} = b/\omega = 17.1\) tokens per forward pass. Each TD pass processes 256 positions instead of one, so it does more FLOPs and its positions touch more experts; Parts 1–5 weigh these costs against the shared read of the weights.

Write \(\Gamma\) for TD's throughput over AR's, in tokens per second, for the same model on the same accelerator, at batch size \(B\) (sequences decoded together) and context length \(C\) (tokens already in each sequence's KV cache, the keys and values that every attention layer stores for the tokens already processed). TD is faster when \(\Gamma > 1\). The answer is not obvious, because a TD pass costs more than an AR step in two ways and less in one:

  • More FLOPs: a TD pass processes \(b\) positions per sequence, an AR step one.
  • More expert weights: in a mixture-of-experts (MoE) model each position selects its own experts, so \(b\) positions read more of them than one token does.
  • Fewer bytes per token: the weights and the KV cache read by a pass are shared by every position it processes.

Which of these dominates depends on whether a pass waits on the accelerator's arithmetic or on its memory, and the roofline model (Williams et al., 2009) is the standard way to decide that: it bounds the time of a pass that does \(F\) FLOPs and reads \(Q\) bytes from memory from below by \(\max(F/\pi, Q/\beta)\), with \(\pi\) the accelerator's peak FLOP/s and \(\beta\) its memory bandwidth; Pope et al. (2022) and Austin et al. (2025) apply it to transformer inference.

What is already measured

The DiffusionGemma technical report (2026, §6) compares DiffusionGemma 26B-A4B with Gemma 4 26B-A4B (Gemma 4 technical report, 2026) on an H100 in FP8, that is, with weights stored as 8-bit floats (BF16 is the 16-bit format). At batch 1, with a 4,096-token prompt, it reports 7.1 times AR's throughput (1,456 against 204 tokens per second, at 19.7 tokens per forward pass), and AR ahead from about 32 concurrent requests. It also counts about 84 distinct experts per layer for a canvas of 256 tokens on PG-19, against 8 for one AR token. vLLM reports 1,288 tokens per second for DiffusionGemma on an H200 in FP8 at batch 1. These are measurements at a few points. The book derives the gain as a function of the batch, the context, the canvas and the accelerator, identifies the resource that bounds each decoder in each regime, and accounts for the difference between this bound and a measurement kernel by kernel, a kernel being one routine launched on the GPU, such as a matrix product or an attention call.

How the book is organised

The book has two halves. Parts 1–7 bound \(\Gamma\) on paper with a roofline model: every gain they report is a ratio of two lower bounds on time, one per decoder. Such a ratio is not itself a bound on the measured gain, which can fall on either side of it (Figure 3 and Proposition 9); "bound" is used throughout for this roofline gain. Parts 8–11 measure \(\Gamma\) for DiffusionGemma on one H200 and account for the difference between the bound and the measurement.

Parts 1–7 derive the gain \(\Gamma\) as a ratio of roofline bounds and map it over batch and context. Parts 8–11 take the inputs of those bounds from measurements, time every kernel of both decoders in vLLM, rewrite the two kernels that lag most, and compare the measured gain with the bound.   • Summary states the results first, so that each later chapter reads as the proof of one of them.
  • Part 1 counts the FLOPs and bytes of an AR step and of a TD pass. Part 2 turns them into Proposition 1, the gain as a ratio of attainable FLOP rates. Parts 3 and 4 evaluate it when the weights and when the KV cache dominate, and Part 5 covers every context in between.
  • Part 6 is a calculator for any accelerator and model, and Part 7 works three variations of the running example.
  • Part 8 measures the two inputs that Parts 1–7 take on trust: the rates of the accelerator and the experts each pass touches. Part 11 puts the bound and the measurement side by side.

From the bound to the measurement

The bound of Parts 1–7 rests on two kinds of input that are not measured there. The accelerator's rates, its peak FLOP/s \(\pi\) and memory bandwidth \(\beta\), come from its datasheet. The number of experts a pass touches comes from assumption A2 of Proposition 1, that tokens choose their experts independently and uniformly. The measured gain then differs from the bound in a third way: real kernels run below their rooflines. Each of the practice chapters accounts for one of these differences.

Figure 3

From the book's bound to the measured gain in four steps.

DiffusionGemma (TD) against Gemma 4 26B-A4B (AR) on one H200, FP8 weights, at two of the 25 measured points. (a) is the book's exact accounting, vocabulary included, with the kernel-by-kernel timing rule of Part 5, at FP8, with datasheet rates and independent routing, evaluated at the context of the measured windows. Each later column changes one input. Part 8 maps (a)–(c) over batch and context, and Part 11 maps (c) and (d).

The two example points show that the differences do not cancel and do not even share a sign. At batch 1 the measured routing raises the bound, in the exact accounting, from 2.7 to 4.2, because a canvas touches fewer experts than A2 predicts, and the measured gain, 7.0, is higher still. At batch 64 every step lowers the gain, from 1.36 to 0.71, until AR is the faster decoder.

The measurements of Parts 8–11 share one setting.

  • Models: DiffusionGemma 26B-A4B (TD) and Gemma 4 26B-A4B (AR), which has the same backbone. Parts 1–7 pair DiffusionGemma with a hypothetical AR twin that runs its own weights; the measurements use the released AR checkpoint, so AR's routing is Gemma 4's.
  • Accelerator: one H200 SXM, capped at 700 W.
  • Engine: vLLM 0.28.0 with CUDA graphs and the fixes of Part 9; FP8 weights, BF16 KV cache.
  • Workload: prompts cut from the PG-19 test books, each followed by a request for a long essay about the passage, in the model's chat template, with TD measured on the third to sixth canvases of its answer; \(B \in \{1, 2, 4, \dots, 64\}\) and prompts of \(C \in \{1\text{k}, 4\text{k}, 16\text{k}, 64\text{k}\}\) tokens with \(BC \le 2^{21}\), so that for the measurements \(C\) is the prompt length and the cache holds about \(C + 1024\) tokens at the middle of the measured windows; a canvas of \(b = 256\) positions with \(S = 14\) denoising passes; sampling at temperature 1; three repeats per point with different prompts. Two TD points, \((32, 64\text{k})\) and \((64, 16\text{k})\), did not fit in memory with the expert kernel of Part 10, so TD has 25 points and AR 27.

How to read it

  • Reading depth: the switch at the top of each chapter folds every definition and proposition to its title and one sentence ("intuition", the default) or shows it in full ("full math").
  • Colours: AR quantities are blue and TD quantities orange, in the equations and in the figures.
  • Units: contexts and batch sizes that are chosen (axes, measured points) are powers of two, with 1k = 1,024 tokens and 1M = \(2^{20}\); thresholds that are computed are in decimal thousands, so \(C_F = 37.6\)k is 37,600 tokens.
  • Three paths: for the results, the Summary, Figure 3 and Part 11; for the model, Parts 1–7 in order; for the measurements, Parts 8–11, of which Parts 9 and 10 are engineering and define every hardware term they use.
