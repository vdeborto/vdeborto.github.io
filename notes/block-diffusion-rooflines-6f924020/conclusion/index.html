Conclusion

Part 11 of Block Diffusion RooflinesPrevious: 10 · KernelsNext: A · Appendix

This chapter sets the measured gain against the bound at measured inputs, shows that their ratio is the ratio of the two decoders' efficiencies, and states what the roofline model gets right, what it needs, and what remains open.

The bound against the measurement

Part 8 gave the bound at measured ceilings and routing, map (c) of Figure 12; Parts 9 and 10 measured both decoders with every kernel attributed and the two worst kernels rewritten. The measured gain can now be set against the bound.

Same scale as Figure 12. Medians of three repeats; the measured gain is TD's tokens per second over AR's at equal \(B\) and \(C\).

Proposition 9The measured gain is the bound times a ratio of efficiencies

In short. The measured gain over the bound equals TD's step efficiency over AR's, so the bound is conservative exactly when TD runs closer to its roofline.

For each decoder \(X\), let \(n_X\) be its tokens per step, \(t_X\) its measured step time and \(r_X\) the empirical roofline of the step at the measured inputs (Definitions 5–7), with \(\eta_X = r_X/t_X\). Let \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{meas}} = (\class{mj-td}{n_{TD}}/\class{mj-td}{t_{TD}})/(\class{mj-ar}{n_{AR}}/\class{mj-ar}{t_{AR}})\) and \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{roof}} = (\class{mj-td}{n_{TD}}/\class{mj-td}{r_{TD}})/(\class{mj-ar}{n_{AR}}/\class{mj-ar}{r_{AR}})\). Then

\[ \frac{\Gamma_{\mathrm{meas}}}{\Gamma_{\mathrm{roof}}} = \frac{\class{mj-td}{\eta_{TD}}}{\class{mj-ar}{\eta_{AR}}}. \]

In particular, the bound underestimates the measured gain exactly when TD runs closer to its roofline than AR, and it is never a bound on the measured gain in either direction.

Proof

The tokens per step cancel: \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{meas}}/\Gamma_{\mathrm{roof}} = (\class{mj-td}{r_{TD}}/\class{mj-td}{t_{TD}})\,/\,(\class{mj-ar}{r_{AR}}/\class{mj-ar}{t_{AR}})\). \(\blacksquare\)

Figure 21

The bound is conservative up to batch 8 and optimistic from batch 16 on.

Measured gain over the bound (c), which equals \(\class{mj-td}{\eta_{TD}}/\class{mj-ar}{\eta_{AR}}\) by Proposition 9. Orange where the measured gain exceeds the bound, blue where it falls short; the shade grows with the distance from 1 on its own log scale.

The ratio is 1.04–1.83 up to batch 8 and 0.79–0.96 from batch 16 on. AR's step efficiency rises faster with the batch than TD's, from 0.27–0.32 at batch 1 to 0.62–0.65 at batch 64, against 0.30–0.42 and 0.51–0.52 for TD: AR's latency-bound small GEMMs, idle time and time outside the model weigh less as its batch grows, while TD's batch is already large in tokens at batch 1. At batch 8 and above the bound is within 21% of the measured gain at every context, which says that the two decoders run at similar fractions of their rooflines, about half, and not that either is close to its roofline.

What the roofline gets right, and what it needs

  • The shape holds. The measured gain falls with the batch, as Parts 3–5 predict: 7.0 at batch 1 and context 4k, 1.05 at batch 32 and context 1k, and AR is faster from somewhere between batch 32 and 64 at short context. Against the context it rises slightly from 1k to 4k and falls from 4k on (1.54 at batch 16 and 1k, 1.19 at 64k).
  • Routing is the input that matters most. Assumption A2 is wrong for both decoders and in opposite directions. The positions of a canvas share experts, which lowers the expert penalty at batch 1 from 7.9 to 2.3; with that alone, Proposition 1 gives 7.9 at batch 1 against a measured 6.95, where A2 gives 2.3. Uneven expert popularity makes AR cheaper at large batch, which moves the parity batch from the Front page's estimate near 118 (BF16, A2, prompt 1,024 and 8,192 tokens generated) to between 32 and 64 here. The book's headline numbers are bounds under A2; the measured ones should be read alongside them.
  • Measured ceilings matter where one decoder is compute-bound. The power cap lowers compute more than bandwidth, so the gain bound loses up to a quarter where TD is compute-bound and AR is not.
  • The ratio depends on equal tuning. By Proposition 9, any kernel that helps one decoder more than the other moves the measured gain by the same factor. TD received two custom kernels and AR none; tuning AR's small GEMMs, idle time and time outside the model would lower the measured gain at small batch, where it now exceeds the bound.

Limitations

  • One accelerator and one pair of models. An H200, DiffusionGemma, and Gemma 4 26B-A4B as its AR counterpart rather than an AR decoder of DiffusionGemma's own weights.
  • One workload. Continuations of PG-19 text at temperature 1, with a fixed \(S = 14\) and every canvas position committed together; quality is checked on GSM8K and IFEval only. Routing, and hence the gain, may differ on other text.
  • Precision. The measurements use FP8 weights; the headline numbers of Parts 1–7 are BF16.
  • Throughput only. Batches run in lockstep at one context; prefill, the time to the first canvas and per-request latency are not measured.
  • Variance. Three repeats per point. At batch 1 the experts a canvas touches vary most between runs, from 23 to 67 per layer at context 1k, so batch-1 results are the least precise.

Related work

  • Diffusion against autoregressive decoding. Kim et al. (2025) combine a roofline analysis with profiling of LLaDA-8B against LLaMA-3-8B: AR models batch better, and block-wise decoding makes arithmetic intensity independent of the generation length. Peng et al. (2025) find AR faster than open diffusion models in most settings, with the gains of caching and parallel decoding confined to small batches. Neither studies an MoE model; this book adds the expert and selection penalties, sparse attention, and a measurement on an MoE model in a production engine.
  • Speculative decoding, the other way to commit several tokens per pass (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023), has the same accounting: verifying a draft is a pass over several positions. MoESD models the experts touched with the same \(U_{E,k}(N)\) under uniform independent routing; Part 8 shows that a canvas violates this assumption. MagicDec finds speculative decoding effective at large batch once the KV cache dominates, the counterpart of Part 4's regime.
  • Parallel decoding with adaptive commits. Fast-dLLM commits positions whose confidence clears a threshold and caches keys and values approximately; dInfer is an inference framework built on such methods. Both make the tokens per forward pass, fixed at 17.1 here, a variable traded against quality.
  • Routing locality. The Mixtral report finds consecutive tokens repeating experts more often than chance in its upper layers, and Liang et al. (2025) measure local routing consistency across 20 MoE models. Part 8 measures a different quantity, the distinct experts of a whole canvas, and finds the dependence strongest in the first layers.
  • Rooflines, power and inference cost. The roofline model is due to Williams, Waterman and Patterson (2009) and its empirical ceilings to the Empirical Roofline Toolkit (Lo et al., 2014). Choi, Vuduc and coauthors extend it to energy and to power caps; Patel et al. (2024) characterise power capping for LLM inference, and Luo et al. (2025) microbenchmark Hopper. Pope et al. (2022) is the standard accounting of transformer inference cost.
  • Kernels. vLLM and PagedAttention (Kwon et al., 2023), FlashAttention 3 and FlashAttention 4, grouped MoE GEMMs such as MegaBlocks and DeepGEMM, and FlashMLA, whose Hopper kernels handle a 512-dimensional value head and were not compared here.

Open problems

  • A grouped expert GEMM of cuBLAS quality, with producer and consumer warps and the fused epilogues of Part 10: the largest recoverable time of TD up to batch 8.
  • Global attention at batch 1, where calls stay on FlashAttention 4 at about half of the attention ceiling; splitting the keys across more CTAs would fill the GPU.
  • An exchange that overlaps compute in the pair kernel, which now waits for the partner's scores before each softmax.
  • Time outside the model, the first recoverable time from batch 16 on: it should be split into named kernels before anything is fused.
  • Equal tuning of AR, so that Proposition 9 compares like with like.
  • Routing per sequence, to test whether AR's excess over independent routing comes from one token per sequence, and why a canvas touches fewer experts at long context.
  • A model of \(N^{\mathrm{eff}}\) in terms of the batch, the context and the step of the denoising schedule, which would let Parts 3–5 use measured routing in closed form.
